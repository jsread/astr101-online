# Astronomical spectra

## Emission spectra

“Fingerprint” of composition, 
emitted by energized gas

Only a few specific wavelengths of light are present in an emission spectrum from an energized gas

![Examples of emission spectra, with bright lines at specific wavelengths forming characteristic patterns for different light sources.](img/emission-spectra-examples.png)

## Element discovery!

The element Helium was first discovered from an emission line spectrum of the Sun’s atmosphere (observed during an eclipse)

![Historical illustration of the solar eclipse of August 18, 1868, showing the dark lunar disk surrounded by a bright rim and extended streamers of the Sun's corona.](img/eclipse-corona.jpg)

The bright yellow line didn't match any known elements.

![Spectrum of the Sun's atmosphere on a wavelength scale from about 400 to 700 nanometers, with several bright lines and a prominent yellow line near 590 nanometers.](img/corona-spectrum.jpg)

## Continuous Spectrum

![Continuous spectrum showing an uninterrupted band of colors from violet to red.](img/continuous-spectrum.png)

A hot, dense source produces light at many wavelengths

Heat energy moves electrons to higher energy levels
Dense matter has many possible energy levels 

(complicated atomic structures, interactions between atoms, free electrons have escaped a single atom)

Hot dense matter produces light in a wide range of wavelengths instead of just a few lines

## Continous astronomical sources

The hot dense cores of stars produce continuous spectra

![The Sirius A and Sirius B stellar system, illustrating stars as sources of continuous light.](img/sirius-a-b.jpg)

Matter falling into black holes produces continuous spectra

![Quasars, illustrating luminous sources powered by matter falling toward black holes.](img/quasars.jpg)

## Absorption

Astronomers use distant hot dense sources to measure the composition of gas in space

![Illustration of using light from a distant quasar to study intervening gas through the absorption it produces in the quasar's spectrum.](img/quasar-gas-observation-1.jpg)

Spectra can be measured from extremely distant objects

![Quasar spectroscopy example illustrating measurements of absorption by gas along the line of sight to a distant light source.](img/quasar-gas-observation-2.jpg)

Note: all stars produce absorption spectra because they have lower-density outer layers

![Diagram of continuous light from a star passing through its outer layers and emerging with dark absorption lines in its spectrum.](img/star-spectra.jpg)


Absorption lines can be recorded even for dim stars

Astronomers first categorized stars by the number of absorption lines

![Comparison of stellar spectra used to classify stars by their absorption-line patterns.](img/line-categorization-of-stars.jpg)

Annie Jump Cannon (1863-1941) and Antonia Maury (1866-1952) analyzed astronomical data.

They realized that the spectral classes of stars could be ordered based on temperature

![Stellar spectra arranged in the spectral-class sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M, comparing absorption-line patterns across the temperature sequence.](img/OBAFGKM-spectra.jpg)

How did they know temperature? Compare the energy (wavelengths) of the light the stars emit!
