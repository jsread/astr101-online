## Orbiting Planets

Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn can be seen by eye.
![A diagram of the solar system, seen from the side, with Mercury, Sun,Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto orbiting the Sun.](img/SlSolarSystemINSC.svg)

Like Earth, the planets orbit the sun counter-clockwise, in nearly circular orbits

![orbits of the inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars)](img/BOSF_01_05.jpg)
![orbits of the outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune)](img/01-mean-distance-outer-planets-715s.jpg)  
*[Source: NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter1-2/)*

It takes Mercury 88 Earth days to complete one orbit, 
but Saturn takes 29 Earth years.

The major planets all orbit close to the Ecliptic plane.

![Tilts of the different planet orbits compared to the ecliptic. They range from Uranus at 0.8 degrees up to Mercury at 7 degrees.](img/Ecliptic+and+orbital+variances+850.jpg)
*[Source: Bryan Simpson](https://www.cinastro.org/planetary-aligments)*

* They appear to lie on the celestial sphere close to the ecliptic (path of the Sun)


* Over one night planets rise in the East and set in the West, along with the Sun, Moon, and stars.

## Planets over many days

* Like the Sun and Moon, planets appear to slowly drift through the constellations of the Zodiac (taking weeks to years)

* Suppose you (from Earth) observe Mars each evening for a few days

* Because [Mars orbits the Sun](#orbiting-planets), its position relative to the background stars will change

* This composite photo shows Mars and Uranus imaged against the same background stars over 16 consecutive nights.

![Looking Sourth. East is left, West is right. This composite image shows Mars and Uranus imaged against the same background stars over 16 consecutive clear nights beginning on July 12. The 16 Mars images sweep in a line across the width of the photo. The 16 Uranus images are close together in a parallel line near one of the Mars images.
](img/observing-mars-uranus.jpg)

* Counter-clockwise orbits (seen from above the North Pole) *usually* carry planets West to East compared to background stars.

* Inner planets move faster than outer planets.


### Observations of Mars in retrograde

* This composite photo is made up of photos of  Mars, against the same background stars, taken over several months.

* We look high in the southern sky to see Mars. So East is to the left, and West is to the right. The earliest day's images are on the right. 


![Mars against background stars, making a looping pattern as described below.](img/retrograde-prograde-annotated.jpg)
*Base image source: [Tunc Tezel via APOD](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap250530.html)*


* Mars starts by moving West to East. We call this prograde motion.

* Then, it moves East to West for a bit. We call this retrograde motion.

* Then, it returns to moving West to East. This is prograde motion again.

## Summary: How we see planets in the sky

* On any one night, planets rise in the East and set in the West.

* If you observe the sky over many nights planets usually appear to drift from West to East compared to background stars (Prograde)

* But sometimes over many nights planets move the other way, from East to West compared to background stars (Retrograde)

As the planets orbit the Sun, the outer planets move more slowly than the inner planets.

Every so often, the inner planet overtakes and passes the outer planet.

This changes the apparent motion compared to distant stars.

Example:
As the Earth passes Mars in it’s orbit, Mars appears to go "backwards" (East to West) for a while.

![An animation showing Earth and Mars viewed from Polaris. Perspective lines go from Earth, through Mars, to the Zodiac stars. Eastward is along the counterclockwise orbit, westward is backward. Usually the arrows show Mars moving forward in its orbit relative to distant stars. As Earth moves faster past Mars, the arrows aim backward for a while. ](img/mars-retrograde.gif)

We also get retrograde motion viewing an inner planet.

![An animation showing Earth's view of an inner planet moving back and forth against the stars.](img/inner-planet-retrograde.gif)

This type of motion through the stars likely inspired the Ancient Greek name for planets - wandering stars.

## Mapping the Sky



Altitude and Azimuth are sky locations measured in degrees.

A lensatic compass can be used to measure azimuth.
![The setting sun viewed through the sight wire of lensatic compass.](img/Setting_sun_in_the_West_(Unsplash).jpg)
*Source: [Tim Graf timgraf99, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Setting_sun_in_the_West_%28Unsplash%29.jpg)*

A sextant can measure altitude.
![Two people on a sailing ship hold sextants and look through the eyepieces.](img/2008_sea_stargazing.jpg)

They are angular distances.

Azimuth goes around the circle of the horizon from North, then altitude goes up from the horizon)

![altitude points up, azimuth to the right. An arrow to the right says "what direction do I look?" an arrow up says "how far up?" and ends on a star.](img/alt-az.jpg)

Altitude:
Increasing “height in the sky” precision with angle measurements
![A viewer looks at different angles up from the horizon: 0 degrees to the horizon, then 30 degrees up, 60 degrees up, and finally 90 degrees up to the zenith directly overhead. The angles are equally spaced.](img/altitude.png)  
*[Source: timeanddate.com](https://www.timeanddate.com/astronomy/horizontal-coordinate-system.html)*

Azimuth:
Increasing compass direction precision with angle measurements
![A viewer looks straight ahead to North on the Horizon, 0 degrees. Angles are spaced evenly around the circle, with East to the right at 90 degrees, South behind the observer at 180 degrees, West to the left at 270 degrees.](img/azimuth.png)  
*[Source: timeanddate.com](https://www.timeanddate.com/astronomy/horizontal-coordinate-system.html)*
